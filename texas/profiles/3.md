  - o 2013–2018: Served as President and Co-Chief Executive Officer of TSMC.
  - o 2012–2013: Served as Co-Chief Operating Officer of TSMC.
  - o 2009–2012: Served as Senior Vice President of Business Development at TSMC.
  - o 2008–2009: Served as Senior Vice President of Mainstream Technology Business at TSMC.
  - o 2005–2008: Served as Senior Vice President of Operations at TSMC.
  - o 1998–2005: Held a series of executive positions across TSMC's fabs and Operations organization, joining the company in 1998 as Vice President.
  - o Pre-TSMC: Served as Senior Vice President of Technology at Chartered Semiconductor Manufacturing Ltd. in Singapore; earlier held the position of Senior Manager for Logic and SRAM Technology Development at STMicroelectronics N.V. in Texas; began his career as a member of the research and development technical staff at Texas Instruments after completing his doctorate.

## PART 2: COMPANY PROFILE

### About Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC)

− TSMC's identity and strategic positioning:
  - o TSMC is the world's largest dedicated (pure-play) semiconductor foundry, manufacturing integrated circuits designed by fabless customers such as Apple, Nvidia, AMD, Qualcomm and Broadcom; it is not owned by, and has no disclosed ownership relationship with, Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund.
  - o As the sole manufacturer of the most advanced logic chips powering the global AI buildout, TSMC's expansion decisions (technology roadmap, capacity allocation, and geographic diversification) are of direct strategic relevance to any sovereign investor evaluating exposure to AI infrastructure and the global semiconductor supply chain.

### 2. Business Description

− TSMC's operations and revenue model:
  - o TSMC is the world's largest dedicated semiconductor foundry, manufacturing custom integrated circuits for fabless chip designers under a pure-play contract manufacturing model it pioneered in 1987.
  - o Its most advanced proprietary process technologies — including N3 (3-nanometer) and N2 (2-nanometer, entering mass production in late 2025) — and its CoWoS/CoPoS advanced packaging platforms are owned and operated exclusively by TSMC, distinguishing it from the customers who own the chip designs it manufactures.
  - o TSMC sells directly to fabless semiconductor companies and integrated device manufacturers through long-term wafer-supply agreements and capacity-reservation arrangements rather than through retail or consumer channels.
  - o TSMC's primary revenue driver is wafer fabrication services, increasingly weighted toward High-Performance Computing (HPC) applications (AI accelerators, GPUs, data-center CPUs), which grew from roughly 36% of revenue in 2020 to approximately 58% of revenue in 2025, overtaking smartphones as the company's largest end-market.

  - o 2024: TSMC began construction of a specialty-technology fab in Dresden, Germany, its first European manufacturing facility.

### 8. Strategy and Future

− Strategic priorities:
  - o TSMC's strategy centers on maintaining process-technology leadership at its most advanced nodes (N3, N2, and future Angstrom-class nodes) to retain its position as the sole large-scale manufacturer capable of producing the leading-edge logic chips required for AI accelerators, positioning High-Performance Computing as its primary long-term growth driver as it displaces smartphones as the largest revenue segment.
  - o The company is directing the large majority of its rising capital expenditure (guided at US$60–64 Bn for FY2026, up from US$52–56 Bn prior guidance) toward capacity expansion for N2 production and toward advanced packaging (CoWoS and next-generation CoPoS), which industry analysts have identified as the binding capacity constraint on global AI chip supply through 2026.
  - o TSMC is pursuing significant geographic diversification of manufacturing away from sole reliance on Taiwan, with an expanded, multi-fab commitment in Arizona (announced 2026, alongside the possibility of additional 2-nanometer-class fabs and a first U.S. advanced packaging facility), continued expansion in Kumamoto, Japan, and its first European fab in Dresden, Germany, aimed at derisking supply for major customers and meeting government incentive commitments (including the U.S. CHIPS Act).

### 9. Controversy

− Notable controversies, in reverse-chronological order:
  - o In July 2025, a discrimination class-action lawsuit against TSMC Arizona was refiled in U.S. federal court with more than 30 current and former employees, alleging "anti-American" bias, workplace harassment, and unsafe working conditions at its Phoenix facilities.
  - o In November 2024, more than a dozen current and former TSMC Arizona employees filed a class-action lawsuit alleging discriminatory hiring and promotion practices favoring Taiwanese and Chinese nationals, workplace harassment (including alleged racist remarks and inappropriate physical contact), and unsafe conditions, including allegations the company sourced safety harnesses from the online marketplace Temu.
  - o In November 2024, Arizona's Division of Occupational Safety and Health (ADOSH) fined TSMC approximately US$16,131 following the May 2024 death of a contracted tanker-truck driver at its Phoenix construction site, citing a failure to maintain a workplace free of hazards likely to cause death or serious harm.
  - o In October 2024, the U.S. Commerce Department was reported to be investigating whether TSMC had manufactured advanced AI or smartphone chips that were ultimately diverted to Huawei Technologies, in potential violation of U.S. export control regulations; by April 2025, reports indicated TSMC could face a fine of up to approximately US$1 Bn in connection with the matter.
