## Why the Last Mile Will Not Be Solved by a Single Machine

**By [Author Name]**

30 September 2026

**Logistics Technology, E-Commerce**

##### Customers Want It Faster. Operators Are Paying for It.

Customers now expect delivery to be fast, convenient and reliable, and they expect it as e-commerce takes 20.5% of global retail sales in 2025, up from 19.9% in 2024, according to eMarketer. Operators are the ones who absorb the cost of that expectation. Last-mile delivery accounted for 41% of total supply chain costs in Capgemini Research Institute's 2019 study, more than double any other category, and every extra order adds a stop to a model built around one driver and one vehicle. [1][3]

Saudi Arabia shows how fast the gap can open. The Transport General Authority (TGA) recorded more than 124 million delivery orders in the fourth quarter of 2025, a 60% increase on the previous year. Volume at that pace multiplies the routes, stops and failed attempts that drive cost. Industry research puts first-attempt failure between 8% and 20%, at an average direct cost near $17.78 per failed package, and attributes 45% of failures to address errors. The customer who wanted speed gets a missed delivery, and the operator pays twice for the same parcel. [2][4][18]

That is why operators are turning to routing software, robots, drones and lockers. Each targets a different part of the problem, and none removes all of it. [3][4]

##### How Technology Is Rewriting the Final Leg

Three variables drive last-mile cost: the number of stops a vehicle completes per hour, the share of parcels delivered on the first attempt, and the labour and vehicle time consumed per stop. Routing software raises stops per hour. Automated collection points raise first-attempt success. Robots and drones replace labour and vehicle time. The table below sets out what each technology does, who is using it, at what scale, and where it stops. [3][4]

| | **Intelligent routing** | **Sidewalk robots** | **Drones** | **Smart lockers and collection points** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **What it does** | Plans routes and dispatches drivers to raise stops per hour | Autonomous ground robots carry short orders along pavements | Aircraft fly light packages direct to the customer | Consolidate parcels at one site the customer collects from |
| **Who is using it** | UPS (ORION) | Serve Robotics | Wing with Walmart; Zipline | InPost |
| **Where and at what scale** | Across the UPS fleet: an estimated 100 million miles and 10 million gallons of fuel cut a year | 2,000 robots in 20 US cities (December 2025); 99.8% completion rate | Extension to 150 more Walmart stores, reaching 40 million+ Americans, with 270+ drone delivery locations in 2027 | 40,000+ automated parcel machines and nearly 33,000 pick-up points in nine European countries |
| **Main benefit** | Proven efficiency gains on existing fleets | Removes driver time from short trips | Speed for light baskets | Removes the failed attempt for a recipient who is not home |
| **Main constraint** | Still needs a driver, so labour cost stays | Customers are food platforms (Uber Eats, DoorDash), pointing to short restaurant orders rather than parcels | Payload of about 2.5 lb (Wing) and 8 lb (Zipline Platform 2); FAA Part 108 rule missed its 1 February 2026 deadline and went to White House review on 10 July 2026 | Customer must travel to collect |

[5][6][7][8][9][19]

The pattern across the four is that the technologies sit at different stages and solve different problems. Routing is proven but incremental. Sidewalk robots work reliably, and a 99.8% completion rate indicates the navigation stack is sound, but their customers are restaurant platforms. Drone networks are growing, yet payload and regulation limit them to light baskets and set the pace of rollout. Automated collection points are the most established at scale, and they trade delivery convenience for fewer failed trips. [5][6][7][8][9][19]

##### Last-Mile Innovation in the GCC

The Gulf is applying the same stack in a different sequence, and regulators lead it. The first layer is the digital address. From 1 January 2026, the TGA requires parcel delivery companies in Saudi Arabia to reject any shipment without a valid National Address, the eight-character short address of four letters and four digits that Saudi Post issues for each building. The TGA frames the rule as a way to speed delivery and cut unnecessary contact between couriers and recipients. It targets address errors, which industry research links to 45% of failed deliveries, with data, not hardware. Aramex offers its My Address verification service in Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the UAE, among other markets. [10][11][12][18]

Automated collection points are the second layer. LLAMA BOX holds a Guinness World Record for 2,072 smart lockers in one location in Riyadh. Omni Llama, a Saudi locker network, had more than 400 lockers in 30 cities and planned to reach 1,000 by the end of 2025. In Bahrain, Aramex and Bahrain Post launched a nationwide network of smart parcel boxes in October 2025. In the fourth quarter of 2025, Riyadh, Makkah and the Eastern Province accounted for 82.5% of Saudi delivery orders, according to TGA data, which concentrates demand where each locker site can serve more customers. [2][13][14][20]

Autonomy is the third layer, and sandboxes govern it. Jahez became the first company licensed under the TGA's regulatory sandbox and runs five autonomous delivery robots at ROSHN Front's business area in Riyadh, each with more than 20 sensors and six cameras. The TGA's land transport strategy targets 25% of goods vehicles being autonomous by 2030. In Dubai, Keeta Drone began commercial flights in Dubai Silicon Oasis in December 2024 with six drones on four routes under the first commercial beyond-visual-line-of-sight licence from the Dubai Civil Aviation Authority, and resumed operations in 2026 after regulatory approvals. The General Authority of Civil Aviation and the TGA carried out Saudi Arabia's first postal drone delivery trial on 4 September 2025. The pattern repeats the American constraint: hardware exists, and licensing decides where it flies. [15][16][17][21]

##### The Winners Will Be the Operators That Combine Technologies

No single technology in the table solves the problem the first section described. Routing does not remove the driver, robots do not carry parcels at scale, drones are limited by payload and licensing, and lockers ask the customer to travel. The future of last-mile delivery will therefore be a combination of technologies, each deployed where it fits the order, the address and the customer. [3][4][5][6][7][9]

Four factors will decide which technologies scale. Commercial viability decides whether a robot or drone beats the cost of a driver on a given route. Customer adoption decides whether recipients will collect from a locker. Infrastructure, from verified addresses to locker networks, decides whether a machine can find the customer at all. Regulation sets the pace of deployment, as the Part 108 delay in the United States and the approvals behind Keeta Drone's return in Dubai show. In the Gulf, where regulators set the sequence, that pace will follow licensing. [10][15][16][19]

Integration is likely to become the competitive advantage. The operators best placed are those that combine address data, collection-point networks and demand data, and then match each order to the cheapest technology that can complete it. Autonomy will arrive in stages, starting with light orders, and the first measurable gains are more likely to come from fewer failed attempts than from new vehicles. [2][13][18]
